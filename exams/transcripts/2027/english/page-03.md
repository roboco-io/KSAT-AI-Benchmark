For decades, companies have treated data like a sealed box — valuable, private, and tightly protected. That instinct made sense. What was inside the box—customer histories, operational insights, proprietary knowledge—represented control and competitive advantage. In an industrial economy, owning data was power. But in the Intelligence Age, that logic is becoming an obstacle. Value no longer comes primarily from keeping information but from connecting it—across functions, partners, and ecosystems—to enable learning and action in real time. Holding on to the sealed box today is less like safeguarding an asset and more like <u>dragging an anchor</u>. The companies moving fastest are not just opening the box. They are doing something more deliberate: opening it just enough to allow data to move, combine, and learn — securely, selectively, and with purpose. The objective is not openness for its own sake but smart connection to gain an edge.

In observation, the best plan is obviously to decide beforehand exactly what needs to be observed, and then to focus attention on this precise point. Reading may be called a kind of observation, since the reader is looking for what the author has to tell; and the rule that holds for other observations holds also for reading. That is to say that the reader finds the most when he knows just what he is looking for. 23. 다음 글의 주제로 가장 적절한 것은?

The environment in which a plant lives is inseparable from the plant itself. The environment informs what kind of plant the offspring will become; the kind that survives and thrives in a challenging scenario or doesn’t. It changes plants’ body plan and possibly directs their development. And those changes can be passed down to their offspring, whose body will develop differently from the start, and be better able to handle the harsh conditions its parents experienced. In other words, parent plants can pass on skills for surviving in a tough world. In some cases this involves whole new body parts and coats of armor. For example, wild radishes that have lived through an attack of destructive caterpillars will make baby radishes with extra-scratchy leaves, and they’ll be preloaded with defensive chemicals to better keep away threats. If these plant-children end up facing the same challenges their parents did, they’ll be much better prepared to handle them.
